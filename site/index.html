Training table generator Česky

Your running season on one sheet of paper.

Write down your races and how much you run now. The generator lays out the weeks up to race day as a printable table: one row per week, a cell for every day. Put it on the wall and colour a cell after each run.

How the sheet works

Each row is a week, Monday to Sunday. The columns on the right say what the week holds: ER, the easy runs (12+12 is two easy runs of 12 km), QR, one quality run, and LR, the long run. The grey number at the end is the week’s planned distance.

H: 6×1′
Hill repeats: six one-minute hard efforts uphill, jogging down between them.
I: 5×3′
Intervals: five three-minute repeats at a hard pace, two minutes of jogging between.
T: 3×8′
Threshold: repeats at a comfortably hard pace.
MP: 3×3 km
Repeats at your marathon pace.
S
A short easy run with a few fast, relaxed strides.

The paces for each of them are printed below the table, worked out from a race you ran recently. You decide on which day each run happens. When you’ve done one, colour its day. The sheet itself is black and white, and the colours are yours; these are the ones the first sheet was coloured with:

  • easy run
  • quality run
  • long run
  • something unexpected: illness, a trip

At the end of the week, colour one box for every kilometre you ran. A week counts as fully done at 80 % of its planned kilometres, so a missed run doesn’t spoil it.

The idea behind it

An outline, not a prescription. No season goes to plan. You get ill, work runs late, you travel. The sheet shows the shape of the season, and you adapt the details as you go. Two rules printed on every sheet say what to give up first:

Short on time: drop an easy run, then intensity, then the long run.

Tired or ill: drop intensity first.

Built on established coaching practice. The rules behind the plan follow well-known coaches and their books: Jack Daniels, Pete Pfitzinger, Greg McMillan, Jason Koop. Three weeks of building, then a lighter week. Quality sessions that grow a little each time they come round. Before each important race, a block aimed at that race, then a taper; after it, time to recover before the training picks up again.

It never refuses a plan. If two races are close together or the season starts late, it still builds the sheet, shortens what has to give, and tells you what to keep an eye on.

Your paces will change. They come from one race result, so they don’t follow your fitness on their own. Check them after each important race and every two or three months: let your easy pace get faster at the same heart rate, but change threshold and marathon pace only after a race or a test run. Then build the sheet again.

Make your own

The generator runs on your own computer, from the terminal. You don’t need to know how to program, but you will type a few commands. It takes about ten minutes the first time.

  1. Install it once

    You need Python 3.11 or newer and Git. Then, in a terminal: 

    git clone https://github.com/f-hluchnik/training-table-generator.git
cd training-table-generator
python3 -m venv .venv
. .venv/bin/activate
pip install -e .

    On Windows, and if something goes wrong, see the set-up steps in the README.

  2. Describe your season

    Create a text file, for example my-season.toml, with your races, a recent race result and how far you run now: 

    start = 2027-05-03              # the first week of the plan

[[race]]
name = "Prague Marathon"
date = 2027-10-10
distance_km = 42.195

[paces]
# a race you ran recently; all paces are worked out from it
race_result = { distance_km = 42.195, time = "3:30:02" }

[load]
easy_run_km = 12                # the length of one easy run
long_run_km = 15                # your long run now

    Add a [[race]] section for every race. Mark each one A (your main goal), B (important, a shorter taper) or C (a tune-up inside your training) with priority = "B".

  3. Build the sheet

    ttg build my-season.toml

    The PDF appears in a folder out next to your file. Anything worth a second look, such as races too close together, is printed in the terminal.

  4. Print it

    On A4, landscape, at 100 % (not “fit to page”), so the cells are big enough to colour. To change the plan later, edit your file and build again.

Overrides for single weeks, hilly races, more runs a week and every other option are described in the README.

The generator is free and open source, on GitHub. It began as one runner’s hand-made marathon plan.

The tables are an outline for healthy, experienced runners, not medical or coaching advice.
